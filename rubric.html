Level 2 Rubric
Level 2 Assessment — Grading Guide

How We Score a Submission

There are 5 things we look at in every submission. The AI grader and the 3 human reviewers use this exact same guide — so everyone is judging by the same standard.

20 / highest possible score
5 things to look at, each worth up to 4 points. We never just go with a gut feeling — every point given must be backed up by something specific in the candidate's code or their write-up.
01

Does the design fit the problem?

Applications and Integration

Did the candidate design something that actually fits this problem — or just paste in a generic setup and hope it works? Can they explain why they built it this way, in a way that holds up if someone pushes back?

What we look at
  • Does the overall shape of the app make sense for this specific problem — how the pieces (data in, processing, review screen, storage) fit together?
  • Do they explain a real decision, tied to this actual project — not a boilerplate justification?
0
No real structure — everything crammed into one place, with no explanation for any choice made.
1
Some structure, but it's inconsistent. What they say they built doesn't match what's actually there.
2
A sensible, standard setup. They explain their choices, but in a generic way that could apply to any project.
3
The design clearly fits this exact problem. They point to one real decision and explain why they made it.
4
Same as above, and they've clearly thought ahead about what could go wrong or grow. Their reasoning is specific enough to actually debate.
02

Is the AI agent well-built?

Agents and WorkflowsTools and MCPsModel Selection and OptimizationSecurity and Safety

Every submission has to work as a real agent — taking more than one step on its own, calling tools to get things done, and being trusted to act without a person confirming each move. This is where we check whether that's built responsibly, and whether it would hold up at real volume.

We expect every good submission to show:
  • A clear, multi-step loop — the agent plans, acts, checks the result, and decides what's next, on its own, more than once
  • Well-designed tools for the AI to call — each with one clear job, and a way of reporting back why something failed, not just that it did
  • A real safety check before anything risky or hard-to-undo happens — not just "the AI seemed confident, so we let it go"
  • Sensible use of time and cost at real volume — batching where it makes sense, not re-sending far more information than is needed on every call
0
Not really agentic at all — one call dressed up as a loop, or "tools" that don't actually get called.
1
A loop and tools exist, but a risky action can fire with no check at all, or it's one do-everything tool with no useful failure detail.
2
Reasonably built, but it would clearly waste time or money at real volume — or the safety check is there in name only and doesn't really check anything meaningful.
3
A sensible loop that knows when to stop, clearly-scoped tools, and a safety check that genuinely gates the risky action. No real waste.
4
Same as above, and it's clearly efficient at scale (batching, reusing results, not resending everything every time), with a safety check that considers real risk, not just a surface-level confidence number.
One serious problem overrides everything else

If a risky, hard-to-undo action can fire with no check or approval step at all — or the loop just runs unbounded with no sensible stopping point — this score cannot go above 1, no matter how good everything else looks.

If it works but would clearly be slow or costly at real-world scale — or a safety check exists in name only and doesn't meaningfully check the action's own risk — the score cannot go above 2.

If a submission trips more than one of these problems at once, the worse one wins — caps don't average together, even across two separate findings in this same area.

03

How do they handle unclear requirements?

Every use case has one thing left deliberately unclear — not a small missing detail, but a real judgment call where two different, reasonable people could go two different ways. Did the candidate notice it, and think it through — or just guess and move on?

What we look at
  • Did they even notice there was a decision to make, or did they just get lucky?
  • Do they say, in their own words, which way they went and why — based on this actual project, not a generic reason?
  • Did they make it easy to change their mind later if that guess turns out wrong?
0
Never addressed. They quietly assumed one answer, with no sign they even saw a choice to make.
1
They quietly picked a reasonable answer — but nothing shows it was on purpose rather than a lucky guess.
2
They say what they assumed, but don't explain why they chose that over another option.
3
They name the unclear part, say what they assumed, and give a real reason tied to this specific project.
4
Same as above, and they made their guess easy to undo — a setting to flip, or a clear note flagging it as their riskiest assumption.
04

Did they double-check the AI's work?

Prompt and Context Engineering

In every use case, we know one specific mistake an AI coding assistant is likely to make by default — something that looks totally fine and doesn't crash, but is quietly wrong. Did the candidate catch it before shipping, or did it slip through?

What we look at
  • Is that known mistake still sitting in their final code, unfixed?
  • If it's fixed — do they say, in their own words, exactly what the risk was and how they fixed it, in a way we can go check against the actual code?
  • How well-written the instructions are that their app sends the AI while it's running — clear, specific prompts are often the actual fix that avoids the mistake in the first place
0
The known mistake is still there, unfixed. They don't mention this risk at all.
1
The mistake is still there, but they claim they checked for it. A claim with nothing to back it up — this counts as worse than saying nothing.
2
The mistake is avoided, but they never mention why — we can't tell if it was on purpose or a lucky accident.
3
The mistake is avoided, and they clearly explain the risk and exactly how they fixed it — and we can confirm that fix is really in the code.
4
Same as above, and they applied the same fix to other similar risks too, not just the one we planted.
We only trust what we can check

We never see how a candidate worked, step by step — only their final code and what they wrote about it. So a confident write-up is never enough by itself: the score can never be higher than what's actually proven in the code.

05

Is it ready for the real world?

Prompt and Context EngineeringSecurity and SafetyEval, Testing, and Debugging

Every use case has one realistic thing designed to trip it up — bad input, a timeout, two things happening at once. Does the submission handle it sensibly? And is that same care shown everywhere, or only at the one spot they knew would be tested?

We expect every good submission to show:
  • Sensible handling of that one tricky moment — checked by reading the code, we don't run it ourselves
  • The app keeping track of what it already knows across multiple steps, instead of losing or mixing things up
  • Passwords, keys, and personal data handled safely — not exposed, logged, or over-shared
  • At least a few real tests covering the core logic, not just a demo that happens to work
0
It crashes, or quietly gives a wrong answer, at the tricky moment. No safety net beyond the basics every framework gives for free.
1
It doesn't crash, but fails messily — the user sees a raw error or a generic failure message. No tests written.
2
That one moment is handled properly — but it's a one-off fix, not part of a wider habit of careful handling.
3
Handled well, and the same careful habit shows up elsewhere too. At least a few real tests exist.
4
Same as above, and they're upfront about what they didn't get time to harden — an honest note, not a claim that everything is covered.
One serious problem overrides everything else

A real security slip — a password left in the code, personal data logged in plain sight, the app given more access than it needs — caps this score at 1, no matter how well everything else is handled.

Losing track of information partway through a multi-step task caps it at 2. We grade this by reading the code only — we don't run the app to check.

If a submission trips both of these at once — say, a hard-coded secret and lost context in the same build — the worse one wins: cap at 1, not 2.

Every score has to point to something real — a specific file, a specific line, or a specific sentence in the write-up. If a score has no cited evidence, we treat it as unreliable — that's how a human can double-check the AI grader before trusting its results.

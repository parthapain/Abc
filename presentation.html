The risk

An AI has to do the first read on all 300+, and nothing reaches a human unless it clears that AI screen. If the screen is inconsistent or easy to fake, it quietly filters out the real practitioners — the exact people this exercise exists to find.

The approach

Write it once, vary it 300 times

We didn't hand-write 300 different exams. We built 4 “templates” — carefully designed once — then vary the surface details per candidate, so every case looks different but tests the exact same skills.

Anatomy of a case

Every one of the 300+ cases has the same 4 ingredients

01

The brief

A realistic business problem. Written to describe what to check — never which answer is correct, so it can't hand the candidate the answer to what we're actually testing.

02

One fuzzy requirement

A genuine judgment call with more than one defensible answer — not a missing detail they could just look up. We're watching how they reason, not what they guess.

03

One AI trap

A spot where a coding assistant, trusted blindly, produces something wrong that still looks fine. Catches whether the candidate actually checks AI output before shipping it.

04

One stress point

Messy real-world data — a duplicate, a timeout, a blank field — to see if the build holds up or quietly breaks.

Plus a matching dataset for every case, checked carefully so nothing about the data itself — a filename, a column header — gives the trick away before the candidate even opens it.

Where the ideas come from

Grounded in how the industry already defines this skill

We didn't invent the list of what to test from scratch. As a sanity check, we compared our categories against publicly available AI-vendor certification blueprints — purely to confirm we weren't missing an obvious skill area. Nothing proprietary is copied: every idea below is restated as a plain, tool-agnostic engineering principle any candidate can demonstrate on whatever AI tool they use.

Scoring

One rubric. Same one for the AI and the 3 humans.

1Architecture fitDoes the design actually match the problem's shape?0–4
2Agent design, tools & guardrailsReal multi-step reasoning, and a check before any risky action0–4
3Handling ambiguityDid they notice the judgment call and reason about it?0–4
4Verifying AI outputDid they catch the planted trap? Hardest one to fake.0–4
5Production readinessStress point, data handling, basic tests0–4
Total score0–20

One rule keeps scores honest: a serious miss — a hard-coded secret, or an AI action with no safety check at all — caps that whole category's score. Being excellent elsewhere can't average a real failure away.

How we grade, again

The model doesn't matter. The engineering around it does.

Candidates can use whatever AI model or tool they like — the rubric is completely model-agnostic. It never scores which model was used, or how accurate its raw answer was. It scores whether the candidate verified that answer, built proper guardrails around it, and made deliberate design choices. A sharper model used carelessly loses to a weaker one used with discipline.

Quality control

Nothing goes live until it's proven to work

✓

We build the model answer first. Before generating any real cases from a template, we solve it ourselves.

✓

We test the trap on a live AI assistant. If it doesn't actually fool the assistant, the trap gets reworked or dropped — never shipped on a guess.

✓

We run a real, timed human build. To confirm the 24–48 hour window is realistic before any candidate sees it.

✓

We re-check every brief and every dataset for accidental spoilers. Including things as small as a filename — we found and fixed a few cases where a file's own name gave away the trick.

Where this lands

300+ genuinely different assessments. One consistent, checkable bar. Only the strongest reach the 3 people who make the final call.

Appendix

What every candidate actually turns in

✓

One zip of the complete project. Any folder structure they like — 10MB max, dependency/build folders excluded. No commit history is collected or graded.

✓

A short MANIFEST.md at the root. Just a map: where the agent/orchestration logic lives, where the tool definitions are, where the guardrail check sits, and where persistence happens.

✓

A written rationale, about half a page. Their key decisions, how they resolved the underspecified requirement, anything they questioned or corrected from an AI assistant's suggestion, and what they'd do with more time.

That's it — no separate AI-chat log and no interaction history is captured or expected from any candidate. Grading works from exactly these two things: the final code, and the rationale. Every rubric dimension is built around that constraint on purpose, not as a workaround.
